What duty cycle means

The one big idea

PWM flicks power on and off very fast. The share of time it stays on is the duty cycle. 0% is off, 100% is full power. The whole project is really just choosing that percentage.

Why it doesn't stutter

The switching is far faster than the motor can react (~20,000 times a second), so it feels a smooth average power and spins steadily.

From knob to duty: the ADC step

The board reads a voltage and turns it into a number from 0 to 4095 (a 12‑bit ADC). You scale that to a duty cycle. Slide the raw value and watch it convert.

duty% = 1843 ÷ 4095 × 100 = 45.0%

Direction: two pins, one motor

Speed came from PWM. Direction comes from two on/off pins (IN1 and IN2) into the driver. One high and one low spins one way; swap them to reverse; both low stops it.

Why a driver at all?

The board can't push the motor

An MCU pin gives a tiny trickle of current — not enough to turn a motor, and a motor dumps voltage spikes back. Wire one directly and you risk frying the board.

The driver does the muscle work

The driver (like a DRV8833) takes your weak signal pins and switches the motor's own battery power. The board decides; the driver delivers.

The golden rule

Give the motor its own battery, and tie the board ground, driver ground, and battery negative together — one common ground. Skip this and nothing works (or worse).

When something breaks

Quick check

1. A higher duty cycle makes the motor…

More on‑time delivers more average power, so it spins faster.

2. What does the ADC do here?

It reads the knob as a number the board scales into a duty cycle.

3. Why not connect the motor straight to a board pin?

Exactly why the driver, with its own supply, sits in between.

4. What sets the motor's direction?

One high, one low spins one way; swap them to reverse.

5. The single most important wiring rule is…

Common ground is what lets the signals and power reference each other.
